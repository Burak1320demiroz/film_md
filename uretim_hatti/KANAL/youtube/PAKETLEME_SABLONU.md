## 5.1 Sabit cevaplar — yorumlara

```
"Is this real?" / "Which mission is this?"
   → No — every frame is AI-generated and hand-curated. The physics is real;
     the mission isn't. No real spacecraft, crew or site is depicted.

"What tools?"
   → Veo for motion, Midjourney for master frames, Google Flow for music,
     DaVinci Resolve for the cut. The discipline matters more than the tools:
     one script, physics-checked scene by scene, same suit and ship in every shot.

"Why is the camera always locked off / single take?"
   → House style. If the camera wanders, you can't tell what moved. Every shot
     is one unbroken take so the physics has to hold in front of you.

"AI slop"
   → Fair test: does it hold? Same suit, same ship, same light in every shot,
     and the physics is checked — point at the frame where it breaks and we'll
     tell you what went wrong.

"Is this a real place on Mars/the Moon?"
   → No. The environment rules are real (pressure, gravity, light); the place
     is invented.
```

**Açıklama:**
```
A routine repair on an airless moon — and the one rule of metal in vacuum she was never trained for.
An astronaut services a derelict lunar installation. When a maintenance task goes wrong, the metal around her starts behaving like nothing on Earth.

THE SCIENCE
• Cold welding is real: two clean metal surfaces in hard vacuum can bond on contact — no heat, no flux. On Earth an oxide skin stops it; in vacuum that skin never grows back.
• Galileo, 1991: the probe's high-gain antenna never fully opened. The investigation traced stuck rib pins to lost lubricant and metal-on-metal bonding in vacuum.
• Spacecraft mechanisms are built from dissimilar metals and coatings for exactly this reason.

**Sabit yorum:**
```
The science behind this one: cold welding. Strip the oxide off two metals on Earth and air puts it back in milliseconds. In vacuum there's no air, so two clean surfaces pressed together simply become one piece. It's one reason the Galileo probe's antenna stayed jammed for its whole mission after 1991 — pins that should have slid, bonded. Every engineer who designs a hinge for space is designing against this. What would you have done the moment the first tool wouldn't come off the rail?
