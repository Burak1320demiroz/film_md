## STEP 3 — CAMERA LANGUAGE PATTERN

- What does the camera DO here: It traps the audience in subjective helplessness, then violently detaches to withhold closure. On the station, it observes the aftermath of a massacre, locking Reese into inescapable, center-punched compositions. On Earth, it transforms into an impassive, static data-reader, observing screens and faces without emotion.
- POV Shift: The coverage favors Dr. Reese's subjective terror in shots 1–14 (marked by handheld wobble, steadicam tracking, and extreme close-ups of her face). At shot 16, the POV completely abandons her, shifting to an omniscient, objective view of the Earth control room (shots 16–27), characterized by sterile, locked-off tripods.
- Recurring setups: Symmetrical, center-punched framing. On the station, the camera repeatedly places Reese dead center within receding, oppressive corridors (shots 5, 7, 8, 10, 11). On Earth, this symmetry is echoed in the massive wide shot of the control room (shot 17).
- Scale strategy: The segment moves from wider, environment-establishing shots to extreme close-ups in both halves. On Earth, it scales down from the massive EWS of the control room (shot 17) to a barrage of static CUs on monitors and the technician's face (shots 24–27), tracking the slow realization of absolute disaster.

## STEP 4 — THE MOST AUTHORED DECISION

The most deliberate choice is the transition between Shot 14, Shot 15, and Shot 16 (01:39–01:55). Instead of showing the monster or cross-cutting the attack with a command center desperately trying to hail the station—a standard coverage plan for a sci-fi thriller—the director abruptly ends a violent whip-pan with a hard, silent cut to pure black. When the image returns, it is an extreme, static macro shot of a steaming, entirely mundane coffee cup. This choice aggressively halts the momentum and starves the audience of narrative payoff, forcing them to experience the profound, uncaring disconnect between the visceral slaughter in space and the bureaucratic boredom of Earth.
