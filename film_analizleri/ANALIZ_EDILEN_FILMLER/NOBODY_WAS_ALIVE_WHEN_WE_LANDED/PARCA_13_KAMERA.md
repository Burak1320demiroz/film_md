## STEP 3 — CAMERA LANGUAGE PATTERN

- What does the camera DO here? It observes with slow, deliberate pushes, emphasizing massive environmental scale over human subjects. It remains securely planted until forced to intrude during a crisis, at which point it becomes unsteadily intimate.
- Whose point of view? Coverage shifts between an objective observer (frequent external tracks) and vehicular subjective alignment (Shots 14, 15). It briefly favors the female character's perspective during her exploration (Shots 4, 5) before switching to a collective group perspective approaching the compound (Shots 16, 20).
- Recurring setups: Symmetrical interior vehicle two-shots (Shots 2, 15) and extreme low-angle dollies framing characters against oppressive, overhead structures (the log in Shot 3; the turret gun in Shots 17, 18).
- Scale strategy: The segment tracks safety versus threat. It shifts from extreme wide shots traversing the jungle (Shots 1, 3) down to tight, handheld close-ups for immediate physical jeopardy (Shots 8, 10), before resetting to a wide scale as the characters approach a new unknown environment.

## STEP 4 — THE MOST AUTHORED DECISION

Shot 10 (01:10–01:21): Instead of cutting to standard over-the-shoulder coverage for the tense dialogue following the attack, the director locks the camera into an extremely tight, symmetrical profile two-shot on the ground. Both characters' helmets are pressed into the edges of the frame, with the threat (the alien plant) visibly out of focus dead center between them. This deliberate framing forces the characters to share the frame, visually unites them in their panic, and keeps the environmental hazard functionally present as the literal centerpiece of their conversation.
