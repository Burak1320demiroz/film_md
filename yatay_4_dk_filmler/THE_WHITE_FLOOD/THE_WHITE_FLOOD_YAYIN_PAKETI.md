### DESCRIPTION (§2) — film-specific lines only

```
The cold here doesn't fall. It fills.

Two people walk down into a basin to pick up one sealed case and carry it back
to their lander. The air at the bottom of that basin is heavier than the air
above it, and it has a surface — a level one, three metres over their heads,
with a rising edge you can watch cross a rock in ten seconds. Everything under
that edge grows white. Everything above it doesn't.

THE SCIENCE
• Cold air pools in low ground. Satellite measurements on the East Antarctic
  Plateau found surface temperatures near −98 °C in shallow hollows — the
  coldest natural surface on Earth — because on still, clear nights the densest
  air drains downhill and settles (Scambos et al., GRL, 2018). The air above
  such a pool is warmer than the air inside it: a temperature inversion.
• Frost does not need a liquid stage. On Mars, frost condenses directly out of
  the air onto the ground and sublimes straight back off it — photographed by
  Viking 2 and by Phoenix.
• Cold traps run far colder than the ground around them. NASA's Lunar
  Reconnaissance Orbiter measured below 40 K inside permanently shadowed polar
  craters — among the coldest measured places in the solar system.
• Low gravity reduces weight, not mass. At 0.62 g a forty-kilogram case is
  easier to hold and exactly as hard to stop.

Everything in this film is one continuous shot per scene. No cuts inside a
shot, no camera moves, no text on screen.
```

The flood in this film is air. Cold air is heavier than warm air, so on a
still, clear night it runs downhill and fills the low ground with a level
surface — the same reason the coldest place ever measured on Earth is a
shallow dip in the Antarctic ice. Everything else here follows from that one
fact. What would you have dropped?
